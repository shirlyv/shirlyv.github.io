### I. The Estuary

The sea at Saltmere does not retreat so much as withdraw its attention. Twice each day it draws off across the flats in a long grey sigh, leaving the creeks gleaming like the veins of a hand, and twice each day it returns, quietly and without apology, to reclaim what it has lent. The villagers speak of it as one speaks of an old relation of uncertain temper: with respect, with wariness, and with an unspoken agreement never to turn one’s back.

Thomas Marsh had turned his back on it for eleven years, and it had permitted him that liberty, as the sea sometimes permits liberties to those it means to keep.

He was the ferryman. The word was a grand one for the work: a flat-bottomed boat, a rope, a pole, and a passage of two hundred yards between the village staithe and the marsh road on the farther bank. But his father had done it, and his grandfather, and the Marshes had kept the crossing since before the church was built, and so Thomas bore the title with a shy, unassuming pride, as a man might wear a medal won by someone else. He was thirty-six years old. He was tall and stooped in the shoulders, with big, tender, cracked hands and a face the weather had made handsome by degrees. He did not talk much. When he did, he said things so plainly true that people went home and thought about them for days.

He drowned on the fourth of November, 1953, in a fog so thick that the lamp on the staithe could not be seen from the middle of the channel. He had gone out to bring over a late passenger, a drover from the marsh farms. The drover was found the next morning on the far bank, half dead of cold but living, and he told the constable that the boat had struck something in the dark, that there had been a sound like a great breath drawn in, and that when he turned to call to the ferryman, the ferryman was not there. The boat was found two days after, drifting upside down at the mouth of the Fleet. Thomas was not found at all.

Eleanor Marsh was thirty-three years old, and for six weeks after that morning she moved through her own house like a woman moving through a house in a dream, touching the backs of chairs, standing for long moments in doorways, unable to recall what had brought her there. There was no body. There was no grave. The vicar read the service over an empty plot beside the churchyard wall, and the village stood in the raw wind with its collars up, and she held her sister Margaret’s arm and felt nothing at all, which frightened her more than weeping would have done.

They had been married nine years. They had had no children. This was a subject upon which she and Thomas had never spoken, and never needed to; it lay between them like a sleeping dog, undisturbed, warm, and not to be mentioned. She had loved him in the way that a person loves the ground beneath her feet, not noticing it until it was gone.

The house stood at the end of Staithe Lane, small and low, with a thatched roof that sagged in the middle like a tired animal and a scullery window that looked out across the water. In that scullery, in the first weeks, she would stand at the sink with her hands in cold water and watch the tide come up the creek, and think: *he is out there*. Not as a metaphor. As a plain fact, like the weather. He was out there, in the grey, somewhere under the long indifferent shine.

### II. The First Letter

The first letter came on the fourteenth of December.

It arrived with the morning post, between a seed catalogue and a notice from the Rural District Council, in a plain cream envelope, addressed in ink to *Mrs. Eleanor Marsh, Staithe Lane, Saltmere*. She stood in the passage with the envelope in her hand for some time before she understood why her heart had begun to beat so oddly. It was the handwriting. It was not quite Thomas’s handwriting, which had been a laborious schoolboy script, round and careful, that leaned backward as if bracing against a wind. This hand was fluid, graceful, and slanted forward. But the capital *E* in her name had the small, peculiar hook at the top that Thomas had always made. She had seen it on a thousand shopping lists.

She opened it standing there in the cold passage. It was dated the twenty-first of December.

> *My dear Nell,*<br><br>
> *Today there was frost. You will have woken early, as you always do when the weather turns, and you will have stood at the scullery window with your shawl over your nightgown and seen that the cold had drawn ferns on the glass, great pale feathers of them, spreading from the corners. You will have put out your hand to touch them and thought better of it.*<br><br>
> *I wanted you to know that I saw them too.*<br><br>
> *I have not forgotten how you hum while you knead. I have not forgotten anything.*<br><br>
> *Your Thomas.*

She read it three times. Then she folded it with great care and put it in the pocket of her apron, and went to the sink, and was violently sick.

It was a cruelty. That was what she told herself, and what she told Margaret on the telephone that evening in a voice so level that her sister was alarmed. Someone in the village had found a way to wound her. Someone had a copy of Thomas’s hand and a grievance, or no grievance at all, only the sour appetite that some people have for other people’s sorrow. She said she would take the letter to the post office and find out who had posted it.

The post office at Saltmere occupied the front room of a flint cottage on the green, and it was kept, as it had been for thirty years, by Mrs. Agnes Cray, a tall, spare, white-haired woman with the posture of a lighthouse and the discretion of a confessional. She took the envelope from Eleanor’s hand and turned it over. She held it up to the window. She examined the stamp and the postmark with a thoroughness that Eleanor found, absurdly, comforting.

“Saltmere, fourteenth of December,” said Mrs. Cray. “That’s today’s date, and that’s our own mark. This went through my hands.” She frowned. “But I did not sort it. I sorted the morning’s bag myself at six o’clock, and there was no cream envelope in it. And it’s not been through the box on the wall, for the box was emptied at five yesterday and I’ve not seen a thing in it since.”

“Then how…”

“I don’t know, my dear.” Mrs. Cray lowered the letter. Something moved behind her eyes, swift and shadowed, like a fish turning under ice. “Tell me. Is there anything in it that’s upset you?”

Eleanor hesitated. “It says it’s the twenty-first.”

“Does it.”

“It speaks of frost.” She did not know why she said it. “As if it had already come.”

Mrs. Cray said nothing at all for a long moment. Then she gave the letter back, gently, and said in an odd voice, “You’ll keep it, I think. And you’ll let me know if there are more.”

### III. The Frost

The frost came on the twenty-first of December, as Eleanor had known with a cold, certain dread that it would.

She woke before dawn. She did not know why, for there was no sound in the house. She put on her shawl over her nightgown and went down the narrow stair and into the scullery without lighting the lamp, and there, in the grey light that came off the water, she saw that the cold had drawn ferns on the glass. Great pale feathers of them, spreading from the corners of the window, each frond edged with a hundred tiny frozen leaves.

She stood there for a very long time.

She put out her hand to touch them, and thought better of it.

That day a second letter came, and a third on the next, and after that they came every morning, at about the hour when the milkman’s float went by, laid upon the mat as softly as a breath. She no longer went to the post office. She understood without being told that Mrs. Cray could not help her, that no one could, and she did not tell Margaret, or the vicar, or anyone. She read the letters in the scullery with her tea going cold beside her, and then she kept them in the oak workbox that had been her mother’s, and tied them in bundles with blue wool.

They were not all prophecies. Most were not. They were such letters as a husband might write who had been parted from his wife by an accident of travel and wished to keep the thread unbroken: small, tender, wandering things. He wrote of the way the light lay on the water in the morning. He wrote of the herons. He asked after the hens. He told her that Farmer Fenner’s ewe would drop twin lambs on the second of January and that one would not live; and on the second of January, in the rain, she heard the news from the carter’s boy, and she sat down upon the stairs and wept, not for the lamb, but because the letter had said, *I am sorry, Nell, I know how you grieve for the small ones.*

He wrote: *I am learning to write. I was never a hand at it, as you know. You used to laugh at my spelling.* And indeed there was not an error in the whole page, not a blot, not a word out of place. The prose was graceful and strange, and it moved her in a way that Thomas’s plain speech never had. For Thomas had loved her silently, with his hands, with the mending of the gate and the cutting of the kindling and the thousand small attentions that she had taken, to her shame, for granted. These letters spoke. They said things she had waited nine years to hear.

*I did not tell you often enough what you were,* he wrote. *I believed that you knew. I see now that no one knows a thing unless it is said.*

She began to wait for them. That was the terrible part, afterward, when she looked back: how quickly the waiting became the shape of her days. She would wake in the dark with a lightness in her chest that it took her a moment to recognise as joy, and she would lie still and think: *there will be a letter*. She dressed with care. She brushed her hair. She was ashamed, and she did it all the same.

### IV. A Stranger’s Tenderness

The Reverend Mr. Pettifer called in the second week of January, and found her very much altered.

“You’re looking well, Mrs. Marsh,” he said, in the doubtful voice of a man who has prepared himself for a house of mourning and finds the curtains open. “I confess I had feared…”

“I am better, Vicar,” she said. “I think I am simply tired of being sad.” Which was a lie, and the truth, both at once.

Her sister was not so easily deceived. Margaret came from Norwich on the twentieth, a stout, brisk woman of forty with a hat like a pudding basin and eyes like a customs officer’s, and she stayed three days, and on the second evening, watching Eleanor lift the poker to the fire with a small private smile, she said, “Nell. Who is he?”

“Who?”

“Don’t be silly. You’ve a look about you. You’ve had it since I arrived. Whoever he is, I don’t say it’s wrong. It’s been two months. A woman’s allowed.” She hesitated. “It’s not the doctor, is it? The new man?”

Eleanor was startled into truth. “Dr. Hale? No. Margaret, no. I’ve spoken to him twice.”

But Margaret had planted the thought, and it grew. She remembered the doctor now, a young man, thirty-five perhaps, who had come to Saltmere in the autumn to take over the late Dr. Pym’s practice. He had called twice after the funeral to ask whether she slept, and had sat in the front room with his long hands loosely clasped between his knees, saying very little and listening with an attention so complete and unforced that she had caught herself telling him things. He was fair and thin and rather shabby, with a scholar’s stoop and an unhurried way of speaking, and he had the habit of looking at the floor when he was moved, which she had found, against her will, touching.

The letters, that week, said:

> *The doctor will ask you to supper on the third of February. He will do it awkwardly, standing in the lane with his hat in his hand. You will say yes. Go, Nell. I am not jealous. I have no use for jealousy. I have only patience.*

She dropped the page and stood very still, with the winter light grey on the scullery wall.

He had never written of anyone but herself. And it was only now, in that cold clarity, that she noticed what she had been carefully refusing to notice: that in six weeks of letters the writer had never once referred to a thing that Thomas had known and she had not. He wrote of her thoughts, her habits, her private weathers of heart, as one who had studied them from a very great depth of attention. But he had never mentioned the ferry. Never the boat. Never the long, slow years of the crossing, or his father, or the pole worn smooth by generations of Marsh hands.

And he had never, once, written the word *drowned*.

### V. What the Letters Knew

On the third of February, in the lane, with his hat in his hand, awkward as a boy, Dr. Julian Hale asked Mrs. Marsh whether she might care to take supper with him on Thursday. His housekeeper, he said, made a respectable fish pie. He understood if the answer were no. He would not press.

Eleanor looked at him. She felt the whole cold weight of the winter sky bear down upon her shoulders, and she heard herself say, in a voice that came from some distance away, “I should like that very much.”

She went home and sat down on the bottom stair and trembled until the shaking stopped.

Supper was pleasant. It was more than pleasant. She found that she was able to laugh, and the sound, unfamiliar, startled her like a bird in the room. Julian Hale told her of his student days in Edinburgh, of his father, a Presbyterian minister who had disapproved of medicine as a form of impertinence toward God, of the long solitary months since coming to Saltmere. He asked about the marshes and listened to her answers as though each were a rare coin. When she rose to go, he said, with his eyes upon the floor, “I hope you won’t think it forward if I say that I have not enjoyed an evening so much in a great while.”

She walked home under a sky of hard, bright stars and let herself in, and on the mat lay a cream envelope, which she carried into the scullery and opened at the kitchen table with trembling hands.

> *You laughed tonight,* it said. *I was glad. I had begun to fear that I should never hear it again. It is a lovely sound, Nell, and he is a good man, and I would not have you alone.*<br><br>
> *But he cannot know you as I do.*<br><br>
> *I have been learning you the way the tide learns a shore: patiently, without haste, one small withdrawal at a time. There is no part of you I have not touched. I know the child.*

The words stood up from the paper like a hand laid upon her throat.

> *I know the child. I know the small grave in the churchyard at Wells, with no name upon it, where you were taken at seventeen to bury what you could not keep. You have never told anyone. Not your sister, nor your mother, nor Thomas, who would have forgiven you with his whole heart if you had given him the chance.*<br><br>
> *I do not ask you to be ashamed. I have no such thing as shame; it is a land animal’s luxury. I ask only that you understand that I have been there, in the darkness, in the water, with every sorrow you have ever drowned. I held it for you. I have it still.*

She sat in her kitchen with the cold lamp and the letter, and she was afraid as she had never been afraid in her life. It was not the fear of a woman who receives a threat. It was the fear of one who has been seen, completely, to the bottom, by something that did not look away.

And beneath the fear, a thing she would never have confessed to any living soul, there was relief. A great, shuddering, shameful relief, like that of a lost traveller who sees at last the lights of a house, and does not stop to ask who lives there.

### VI. Seven Nights

February passed in a slow blur of frost and tide. She dined with Julian twice more. She did not speak of the letters, and he did not ask; but she saw him observing her sometimes, in that quiet way he had, with a physician’s concern and something warmer beneath it, and she knew that he was waiting, as the patient wait, for a word she could not give.

The letters changed.

They grew longer. They grew more urgent, more intimate, and their prophecies stopped being small. He foretold the storm of the ninth of March, which took the roof from the Fenners’ barn. He foretold the death of old Mr. Lamb of the Anchor, three days before it happened. And in the middle of March, on the day the first celandines opened along the dyke, a letter came whose last lines stopped her heart:

> *I have been patient, my love. I have been so very patient. But I find that I am not made for waiting, whatever I have told you; it is a quality I borrowed, as I have borrowed so much, and it wears thin.*<br><br>
> *The spring tides are on the twenty-first. There will be a great one at ten minutes past midnight, the highest in forty years. It will come up past the staithe and over the lane and lay itself against your door. I would have you meet it.*<br><br>
> *Come to the landing. I have kept your place.*

She went at once to Mrs. Cray.

It was past closing, and the post office was dim and smelt of sealing-wax and cold tea. Mrs. Cray sat by the empty stove with her hands in her lap, and when she saw Eleanor’s face she rose, locked the door, drew the blind, and said, “How many?”

“Ninety-one,” said Eleanor. “I counted. I have them all.”

“Yes,” said Mrs. Cray softly. “That would be about right.”

She took a key from her apron pocket and opened the small deal cabinet behind the counter. From it she drew a bundle of letters, yellowed, tied with a faded black ribbon, and laid it in Eleanor’s hands. “These came to my sister Ruth,” she said, “in the autumn of nineteen-fourteen. Her husband went out with the herring boats in September and did not come back. He was the finest man in the village. She never married again. She would not look at another.” She paused. “They were dated ahead, every one of them. The last was dated the night of a spring tide in March of nineteen-fifteen. She went down to the staithe to meet him.”

“And?”

“And the sea took her,” said Mrs. Cray. Her voice was very steady. “Two days later they found her on the sand with her face to the sky and the sweetest look upon it I have ever seen on a living thing, or a dead one. As if she had been welcomed.”

Eleanor sat down upon the nearest chair. The room spun gently.

“It isn’t him,” she said. “Is it?”

“I don’t think it was ever him,” said Mrs. Cray. She took Eleanor’s hands in her own, thin and cold and strong. “I think it takes what a woman loves best, and learns it, and wears it, the way a hermit crab wears a shell. It has no malice in it, child. That’s the pity. It loves in earnest. It loves as the water loves a shore, which is to say it will have it all, and the shore will not survive the loving.”

“Then why did Ruth go?”

Mrs. Cray looked at her for a long time. “Because it was kind,” she said at last. “Because no one else had ever been so kind. Because it said the thing she had waited all her life to hear, and she could not bear to be left again.”

The clock on the wall ticked into the silence, like a heartbeat.

“You’ve seven nights,” said Mrs. Cray. “I should use them well.”

### VII. The Doctor’s Vigil

She told Julian Hale on the sixteenth, in his own consulting room, with the oak workbox on her knees and the lamp turned low.

He heard her to the end. That was the thing she remembered afterward and was grateful for: that he did not interrupt, did not smile the small professional smile of the man who has already decided what is wrong. He held out his hand for the letters, and read them one by one, slowly, with his thin face growing paler in the lamplight. When he had finished the last he sat for a long while with the pages loose in his lap.

“Eleanor,” he said at last, very gently, “I must ask you something, and I want you to know that I ask it as a friend. Do you ever find that you have lost an hour? That you wake in a place you do not remember going to? That your fingers are stained, perhaps, with ink?”

She understood him instantly. “You think I have written them myself.”

“I think grief is a stranger country than we allow, and that the mind will sometimes build a road across it,” he said. “It would explain the handwriting. It would explain the things the writer knows. A mind is a very deep well, my dear, and it contains more than we are aware of.”

“And the frost? The lambs? Mr. Lamb’s death?”

He was silent. Then he said, with the honesty she was coming to love in him, “I do not know. But I would rather doubt the universe than doubt you.”

It was the kindest thing anyone had ever said to her, and she wept, and he held her, and for a moment in the cold lamplit room she thought: this is also love. This is also a way of being known.

He came to Staithe Lane that night and sat in the scullery with his Bible-black medical bag at his feet, a flask of tea, and the stoic patience of a man who has sat up with a great many dying people. He meant to prove, if he could, that no one entered the house; that the letters came from within. She slept, finally, upon the settle by the fire, and when she woke at five he was standing at the door with his face bloodless in the first grey light.

“It came under the door,” he said. “I did not move from this chair. I watched the sill. At twenty minutes to five there was a movement as of a hand, a flat, slow, patient hand, and the envelope slid across the floor, and it was…” He swallowed. “It was wet, Eleanor. It was wet at the edges, and it smelt of the sea.”

The letter was addressed, in that graceful, forward-leaning hand, to *Dr. Julian Hale*.

> *Doctor,*<br><br>
> *Thank you for sitting with her. You have a good heart, and I do not say so lightly; I have measured a great many.*<br><br>
> *You will not stop her. You must not think it a reproach. It is only that I have never been wrong, and it would be unkind to let you hope.*<br><br>
> *On the night of the twenty-first you will reach the landing at nineteen minutes past midnight. You will be nine minutes late.*<br><br>
> *I am sorry. I should have liked to be your friend.*

Julian laid the letter on the table. He said nothing for some time. Then he took off his spectacles, which she had never seen him do, and rubbed his eyes with the back of his wrist, and said in a quite different voice, “Well. I confess I am out of my depth.”

That night, with his help, she burned the letters.

They made a fire in the scullery grate, and she fed them in one by one, ninety-two now, and watched the cream paper brown and curl and blacken, and the forward-leaning hand dissolve into smoke. It was done by midnight. They sat by the ash and did not speak. At dawn she went to the mat, and there, in the grey light, lay the whole of them, every page, unscorched, uncreased, the blue wool tied as she had tied it. On top lay a new letter, a single line.

> *Do not be afraid, Nell. It was not unkind of you. I should have done the same.*

### VIII. The Landing

On the twenty-first of March the sky over Saltmere cleared at sunset, and the whole village, which does not like to speak of such things, nevertheless found reasons to be indoors by dark.

They ate supper in the scullery. Julian had taken the afternoon to arrange what he called, with dry humour, a sensible defence: a rope, which he proposed to tie between their wrists; the loan of the constable’s truncheon; and, in the lane, the doctor’s own motor-car with the engine idling, in case they should need to flee inland. Eleanor had agreed to everything. She had been perfectly calm since the morning. She had baked bread, and while she kneaded it she had found that she was humming.

At a quarter to eleven, upon the table between the salt and the lamp, a cream envelope appeared.

Neither of them had seen it arrive. It was simply there, as a sudden weight appears in a pocket. Eleanor picked it up, and Julian did not try to stop her.

> *My love,*<br><br>
> *You will not need your coat.*<br><br>
> *I should tell you that I have never found it easy to say what I am. I do not think that there is a word. I was here before the village and before the church, and I have taken many shapes, and loved many of the people who stood on this shore. I do not take them to be cruel. I take them because they are the only ones who ever looked at me and did not look away.*<br><br>
> *You looked, Nell. You stood at the glass and watched me for eleven years, and you thought you were watching a man.*

A cold cord of understanding drew tight about her heart. She read the last line again.

“Where is Thomas?” she said aloud, to the empty room.

The answer came, as it must have been written long before, in the very next paragraph:

> *He is with me. He went in gladly; do not grieve for that. He said to me, in the dark, as the boat went over: look after her. And I have tried. I have tried with all my strength. I learned to write because you wished to be written to. I learned to speak because you wished to be spoken to. I have never been so changed by anything in all my existence as I have been by you.*<br><br>
> *Come to the landing. Come and see him. Come, and be known to the end.*

The clock in the passage struck eleven.

Julian said, in a voice that she would hear for the rest of her life, “You are going, aren’t you.”

“I have to see,” she said. “Julian, I have to. If it is Thomas… if there is any part of him…”

He was quiet. Then he took the rope from the back of the chair, and looped one end round his own wrist, and held out the other to her. “Then I shall come with you,” he said. “I cannot stop you. I have been told as much. But I may be allowed to stand beside you.”

They went out by the front door into a night of such stillness that the stars seemed to ring.

The tide had already come over the lane. It lay across the cobbles in a bright black sheet, perfectly smooth, and it did not rush or clamour. It attended. They waded ankle-deep through the silent mirror of it, the stars drowned beneath their feet, down toward the staithe where the lamp stood unlit upon its pole. The air tasted of salt and iron. The village behind them was dark. Not a window showed light; every curtain was drawn, and Eleanor knew, with the same sure, cold knowledge that had given her the frost, that behind each one a person was listening and not breathing.

The landing steps went down into the water, nine of them, worn hollow by generations of Marsh feet. The water stood at the fifth. Where it met the stone, a thing was standing.

It wore Thomas’s coat, the old oilskin with the broken second button. It stood with its hands loose at its sides, a little stooped, in the exact attitude of a man waiting for a passenger. But when it raised its head, the face that turned toward them was not a face at all, not quite. It was the idea of a face, the hush of one, as though someone had taken everything gentle that ever lived in a human countenance and drawn it, very faintly, upon the surface of the water. It was the saddest and most patient thing she had ever seen. It looked at her as no one had ever looked at her, entire, without defence, without a flicker of judgment, and she understood in that instant how Ruth Cray had gone down those steps with a smile.

“Nell,” it said.

The voice was the voice of the letters, soft and low and forward-leaning. It was not Thomas’s voice. It was better than Thomas’s voice, and she hated it for that, with a clear, sudden, dazzling hatred, and loved it too.

She stepped down. The cold stone took her shoe. The rope drew taut at her wrist.

“Eleanor,” said Julian, quite softly, behind her.

“I know,” she said.

“I will not pull you. I said I would not. But I should like to say one thing.” His voice shook, and he steadied it. “I am a plain man. I have no gift for letters. I cannot tell you in advance what will happen, nor what you are, nor what you mean to me, because I do not know it, any of it, the way that… that… appears to. I only know that each morning I wake and wonder whether you will let me bring you tea.”

The thing in the water turned its soft, terrible face toward him.

“He will be nine minutes late,” it said gently. “He is already nine minutes late. It is nothing against him.”

“You told me,” said Eleanor, “that you have never been wrong.”

“I have not.”

“You said that no one knows a thing unless it is said.”

“I did.”

She looked down at the water. She saw her own face in it, and the stars, and under both of them, very faint, the lights of a drowned and lovely house where a man she had loved for nine years sat at a table, mending a gate. She heard the clock in the passage of her heart. She thought of the unmarked grave at Wells. She thought of the scullery window, and the ferns, and a hundred mornings of waiting. And she thought, with an astonishment that was almost like laughter: *Thomas never wrote me a letter in his life.*

“Then I shall say a thing, and you shall know it,” she said. “I am going to live.”

The water stirred.

“I loved him for the gate,” said Eleanor. Her voice rang out, clear and young, across the drowned lane. “I loved him for the kindling and the rope and the pole worn smooth. I loved the man who could not say it. You gave me the words I wanted, and I thank you, I do, from my heart. But words are not what he gave me. He gave me a life, and a life is not a thing one may be known to the end of, it is a thing one must keep on making. You have never been wrong. Then be wrong, once, for me.”

She took one step backward, up out of the water, onto the stone of the sixth stair. Julian caught her hand.

For a long moment nothing moved. Then the face upon the water broke, not in anger, but as a sorrow breaks, softly, from within, into a thousand widening rings. The coat folded. The shape sank. Out across the flats, the vast bright sheet of the tide drew a single deep breath, that same breath the drover had heard on the night of the fog, and let it go.

The water withdrew.

It went down the stairs, one by one, slow as a sigh, and the lane lay bare and wet and shining in the starlight, and in the village behind them a single window lit, and then another.

Julian looked at his watch. His hand was trembling. “Twelve nineteen,” he said, in a hoarse, dazed voice. “I am nine minutes late.”

And Eleanor, to her own great surprise, began to laugh.

### IX. First Frost

The last letter came to the mat in the morning.

> *I have never been wrong,* it said, *and I note with wonder that I find I am glad.*<br><br>
> *I shall not trouble you. I know the weight of a thing unwanted; I have been it. But I should be grateful, if you will permit me, to keep the habit of the thing. Once in each year, upon the morning of the first frost, I shall write.*<br><br>
> *You need not read it.*<br><br>
> *I have the patience of water.*

She married Julian Hale at Whitsun. They had two children, a girl and a boy, and she named the girl Ruth. The doctor grew old in Saltmere, stooping, shabby, adored, his long hands more and more like his father’s, and when he died in the winter of 1991, in his own bed with Eleanor beside him, she held his hand and said, “I have known you thirty-seven years,” and he said, “I should like to have gone on knowing you,” and these were the last full sentences he spoke.

The letters came every year.

She did not always read them. For the first ten winters she laid them unopened in the oak workbox, and the box grew heavy. Then one frosty morning, with the children grown, she sat down in the scullery and opened one, and found that it was only a letter: no prophecy, no summons. A few lines, tender and curious, about the herons, about the light on the flats, about a child who had learned that day to ride a bicycle in the Fenners’ lane. It asked after her health. It asked after her hands.

She wrote back, once, and left the page upon the mat, and in the morning it was gone.

So it went for forty years. Not a courtship now, nor a haunting, but a correspondence, the oddest and most faithful of her life, between a woman who aged and a thing that did not. Mrs. Cray died. The post office closed. A new road was built across the marsh, and the ferry was forgotten, and the boys of Saltmere stopped fishing from the staithe. Still, on the morning of the first frost, a cream envelope lay upon the mat, and Eleanor Hale, in her seventy-third year, with her hair gone white and her hands knotted like rope, would carry it into the scullery and read it with her tea.

The frost came late in the year of 1994. It came on the nineteenth of November, in the dark, a still, clear, glittering night, and she woke before dawn without knowing why. She put on her shawl and went down the stair and found, on the mat, a cream envelope addressed in that graceful, forward-leaning hand.

She opened it in the passage.

It was dated the twentieth of November.

> *My dear Nell,*<br><br>
> *Tomorrow there will be frost. You will wake early, as you always do when the weather turns, and stand at the scullery window with your shawl over your nightgown, and see that the cold has drawn ferns on the glass.*<br><br>
> *I have kept your place.*<br><br>
> *I have kept it forty years. It is a little worn at the edges; I have sat in it often, thinking of you. But it is yours, and it is warm, and he is here. He is quite himself again. He asks whether the gate still holds.*<br><br>
> *There is no hurry. There never was. Come when you are ready.*

She stood quite still for a long time in the cold passage, with the paper in her hand. Then she folded it with great care, and put it in the pocket of her apron, and went into the scullery.

The window was white with ferns, great pale feathers of them spreading from the corners, each frond edged with a hundred tiny frozen leaves, and beyond them, grey and vast and shining, the estuary lay breathing in the early light. She was not afraid. She had not been afraid for a very long time.

She put out her hand to touch the glass.

And this time, she did not think better of it.
