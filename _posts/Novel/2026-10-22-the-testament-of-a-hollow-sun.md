# THE TESTAMENT OF A HOLLOW SUN
*A Novel by Shierolive*

---

### PROLOGUE

Here beginneth the Testament, as set down by Brother Ansgar of the Third Cloister of Solhaven, in the one thousand one hundred and eighteenth year of the Sun, being the fifty-sixth winter since the sealing of the Chest.

It is the custom of chroniclers to open their labours with the glories of kings. I shall open mine with a lock.

For fifty-six years a coffer of black yew-wood, bound in three bands of tarnished silver, lay beneath the roots of the Veyrwood, in the keeping of a lady whom the Aelvar named the Warden of the Last Hour, and whom the common folk, who loved her better than they understood her, called the Queen of the Trees. She is dead now, these eleven months. Her household testifies that she passed in the grey of morning, while a wind stirred the leaves of a wood in which no wind had been felt for three days; and in the week of her burying the Council of Merit, sitting in full conclave in the Gilded Meridian, gave me leave to break the seals.

I record these things plainly, for the age in which I write has grown fond of marvels and careless of dates. I have laboured to fix each event to its day and its hour, so far as the records of the realm and the memory of the Aelvar allow; and where the hour is lost, I have said so. A chronicle that cannot tell when a thing was done will soon be unable to say whether it was done at all.

Know, then, that the Kingdom of Aurendel, which lies between the Ashen Marches in the east and the salt cliffs of the western sea, was governed for a thousand years by the Sun-Kings, whose emblem was a ring of gold upon a field of azure, and whose law declared that none but a man of unmixed blood might wear the crown. That emblem hangs yet above the doors of the Gilded Meridian. It is a hollow thing: a circle with its centre cut away, that the light of the true sun may pass through. Whether the first artificers meant it as a prayer or a mockery, the annals do not say.

Of the persons who stand at the heart of this history, three are known to every child of Aurendel: Claus, called the Hero of the Marches; Chalid, the King of Sand; and Claire, Lady of the Wood. A fourth, whose name the schoolmen omit and whose death began all that follows, was named Cyra.

Within the coffer I found no crown, no charter, no treasure of the kind that sets men to murder. I found a single quire of vellum, written in a hand that grew less steady with every leaf; and upon its first page, beneath a scattering of fine grey grains such as are found nowhere in Aurendel, a single line, which I copy here at the head of all that follows:

> *Let it be written as it was, and not as it was forgiven.*

I have therefore set down the history entire, from the fall of the last Titan to the closing of the Chest, according to the records of the realm, the testimony of the Aelvar, and the Testament itself. Where these disagree, I have said so. Where they are silent, I have been silent also. And where the heart of a man is concerned, which no annal can reach, I have asked the reader’s patience, and trusted it to be given.

## Book the First: The Hero Who Came Home
*Autumn and Winter, 1041 A.S.*

### Chapter I: Of the Last Titan

The ninth day of Amberfall, in the one thousand and forty-first year of the Sun; from first light until the eleventh hour of the morning; upon the Ashen Marches, forty leagues east of Harrowford.

*From the Lay of the Ashen Marches, as it is sung in the taverns of the east:*

> *When Gorrath rose, the sun grew dim,*  
> *And grey the dawn, and grey the hymn;*  
> *But one man stood where none would stand,*  
> *And spared the least in all the land.*

In the autumn of the one thousand and forty-first year of the Sun, upon the ninth day of the month called Amberfall, the morning came up over the Ashen Marches like a coin of pewter drawn slowly from a dirty purse. Amberfall is named, in the speech of the old calendar, for the light that falls golden through the turning leaves; but upon the Marches no leaf had turned in sixty years, for no leaf grew there, and the light that fell upon that country was the colour of dishwater, and lay upon the plain without warmth or promise. The wind, which never ceased in that region, carried the grey dust of the burned fields from one horizon to the other, and the soldiers who kept the eastern line were wont to say that a man might taste the Marches for a week after he had left them.

The country had not always been so named. The older maps, drawn before the coming of the Titanspawn, call it the Golden Reaches, and record there a land of barley and orchard, of mills upon quick streams and bell-towers in every hollow. But the Titanspawn came up out of the fissures of the earth in an age now half forgotten, and burned the fields to grey cinder, and the new name followed the ruin as a hound follows its master. Of these creatures the learned distinguish two sorts. The first are the Titanspawn proper, beasts of such stature that a company of horse may pass beneath their bellies, clad in plates of slate-coloured hide that turn the arrow and blunt the spear. The second are the Lesser Brood, their hatchlings, which in the first weeks of life are no larger than a hunting hound, and are held by the wise to be of gentler temper than their sires, though few soldiers had lived long enough to test the opinion.

Upon that morning there lay in the Marches the last of the former kind: a beast the soldiers called Gorrath the Unburied, because it had slept sixty years beneath a barrow of its own ash, and rose, when it rose, as the dead are said to rise upon the Last Day, unwilling and without mercy. Against it, upon a low ridge of fused rock, stood eleven hundred men of the Eastern Levy (the muster-roll says nine hundred and forty; but muster-rolls are written by quartermasters, who are paid by the head), in dented mail and borrowed courage, under a sergeant named Bram Corvane, a thick-necked veteran with a beard like a wire brush and a voice that had been ruined by thirty years of shouting into wind. Upon the northern ridge, in a long grey line, stood the Aelvar archers, lean and silent, their bows unstrung against the damp, their captain, Sylwen Thornwarden, sitting upon a boulder with her chin in her hand as though she were watching a play she had seen before.

A boy of seventeen, whose name was Pell, and who had been a ploughman until the spring, plucked at the sergeant’s sleeve.

“Sergeant. Is it true what they say? That the Lord Claus goes out alone?”

“It is true that he will go out first,” said Corvane, without turning his head. “Whether he goes out alone is a matter of how many of you can run faster than you are afraid. Stand up straight, boy. You look like a man waiting to be buried.”

“He has no armour but what he stands in,” said Pell. “No shield. No banner. Does his sword have a name, Sergeant? The great ones all have names.”

Corvane gave a short, dry sound that might have been laughter in another country. “The great ones have names because someone else had to give them, lad. This one has a use. Be content with that.”

The man of whom they spoke stood at the foot of the ridge, apart from the host, and was at that moment doing something that no hero of the ballads has ever been shown doing: he was tightening the strap of his left greave, which had worked loose, and swearing quietly under his breath at a buckle. His name was Claus, in the thirty-ninth year of his age. The heralds could name no house for him; he was reared, so men said, by a smith in the western hills, and had risen by no means but the sword. His hair was dark, and had begun to grey at the temples; his face was weathered, and not handsome, and rather tired; and the blade at his hip was a long plain thing of grey steel, with a leather grip worn black by his hands, and no mark upon it of any maker.

When the buckle was mended he straightened, and looked up the slope, and found the sergeant watching him.

“Sergeant.”

“Lord.”

“If I fall, you will not come after me. You will take the men back to the river, and you will burn the bridge behind you. Do you understand?”

“I understand it, Lord. I have not said I will do it.”

“That is the first honest disobedience I have been offered in a month,” said Claus, and for the space of a breath something like a smile touched the corner of his mouth. “I shall take it as a kindness.”

He turned, and walked out onto the plain, and the host behind him began, very softly, to sing. It was an old marching rhyme of the Levy, unlovely and without art, and it was sung as soldiers sing when they are afraid and wish to give the fear a rhythm.

> *Hold the line and hold your breath,*  
> *Grey is the field and grey is death;*  
> *Brother on left and brother on right,*  
> *Sing till the morning, sing till the light.*

### Chapter II: Tremble Black

The ground began to tremble at the second hour after dawn, when the camp-bell of the Levy had but lately rung eight. It was not at first a sound but a pressure, as of a great hand laid upon the world and slowly closing; the pebbles danced upon the stones; the ash lifted from the plain in a long sigh and hung unmoving in the air. Then the barrow split. A crest of black horn rose out of it, as tall as a church-spire, and beneath the horn a head like an overturned boat, plated in grey scale, whose eyes opened one after the other, slow and yellow, like windows lit in a house of the dead. Gorrath drew its first breath in sixty years, and the breath smelled of sulphur and old iron; and when it let that breath go, it was a roar that cracked the stones of the ridge, and sent a hundred crows wheeling up from the dead orchards like scraps of burned paper.

Upon the northern ridge, Sylwen Thornwarden rose from her boulder, and strung her bow.

“Now,” she said, in the soft tongue of her people, and the Aelvar, with one motion, did likewise.

The annals of the war-camp record that the battle endured three hours, and the chroniclers of the Aelvar, who reckon such matters more precisely, say that it was three hours and a quarter. In the first hour the arrows fell like a black rain upon the joints of the Titan’s armour, and were answered by blows of its tail that sheared away the face of the ridge as a man shears a cheese; and Claus, running beneath the shadow of its belly, cut at the tendon of its hindleg, and was flung twenty paces by the recoil, and rose, and ran again. In the second hour the beast, wearying of the arrows, turned its great head and breathed a gout of yellow flame upon the Aelvar line; and the archers, who had been raised in the deep forest and feared nothing so much as fire, did not break, but loosed their last arrows into the open mouth, and withdrew in order, carrying their wounded.

“He fights,” said Sylwen to her lieutenant, watching the small dark figure that darted about the colossus like a spark about a bonfire, “like a man who has something to go home to. That is a dangerous kind of man. They do not know when to die.”

In the third hour Claus climbed. He found a foothold in the notched plating of the foreleg, and went up it as a man climbs a fallen tower, hand over hand, while the whole vast body heaved and shuddered beneath him, and the soldiers upon the ridge, who had ceased to sing, saw him dwindle against the grey flank until he was no larger than a fly. He gained the shoulder. He gained the neck. Gorrath, feeling him there, reared, and flung back its head, and for one moment the sky was full of the black horn; and in that moment Claus drove his blade into the soft hollow beneath the jaw, where the plates part, and held it there with both hands and all his weight, while the beast screamed and the ground shook and blood the colour of tar ran down the length of the steel and over his wrists.

Then Gorrath the Unburied fell, at the eleventh hour by the camp-bell, when the pewter sun stood at its highest and gave no heat. The earth gave a sound like the closing of a great door, and the ash rose in a pillar so tall that it was seen from the walls of Harrowford, forty leagues away; and in the silence that followed, the Levy, which had been standing with its mouth open for the better part of an hour, discovered that it had no breath left in it, and sat down, one man after another, in the grey dust.

It was Pell, the ploughman, who first saw the clutch.

In the hollow of fused glass behind the carcass, where the barrow had been, there lay a score of hatchlings, newly come from the shell, their scales still soft as wet leather, their eyes the colour of dull amber. They were each no larger than a hound. They had not yet learned to fear, and so they did not flee; they huddled together in the ash and looked up at the man who came limping down from the fallen colossus, with his blade yet red and his hands black to the elbow, and they trembled.

“Kill them,” said Pell, in a high strange voice, and his hand went to his sword. “They are the Brood. They will grow. Lord, they will grow, and my father’s farm—”

“Let go of the sword, Pell,” said Corvane, very quietly, and closed his big hand over the boy’s wrist.

Claus stood over the hatchlings for a long time. They were so small. One of them, the smallest, which had a notch in its ear where the shell had cut it in hatching, crept a little forward upon its belly, and laid its brow upon the ash, and made a thin sound like a hinge. Claus lowered the sword.

What followed is attested by the Aelvar, who alone among the witnesses understood the broken speech of the Brood. Sylwen Thornwarden had come down from her ridge, with six of her archers behind her, and she stood now at the man’s shoulder with her head bent, listening to a thin, rasping chorus that rose from the hollow, and rendering it as it came, line by line, into the common tongue.

> *We will not eat the bread of plains,*  
> *Nor wake the sleepers in their chains;*  
> *We keep the peace of him who spared,*  
> *Though none before us ever dared.*

“They are bowing to you,” said Sylwen, when it was done. “They are offering you an oath. It is not a small thing. The Brood do not swear as men swear; they swear once, and they swear with the blood, and they do not forget.”

“Tell them,” said Claus, hoarsely, “that I did not spare them for a promise. I spared them because they were small.”

The Aelvar captain looked at him for a long moment with her grey, unreadable eyes. “That,” she said, “is why they will keep it.”

The chroniclers call the Oath of the Ashen Marches, and they mark it as the first of many oaths in this history. It was to be kept, as shall be told in its place; and it was to be kept, moreover, at a price that no soldier in that grey field could have imagined.

Then Sylwen did a thing that Levy did not understand, and that Claus himself did not understand until long afterward. She stepped back, and lifted her bow above her head in both hands, and the Aelvar behind her did the same; and upon the northern ridge, one by one, the whole grey line took up the gesture, until a thousand bows were raised against the pewter sky. It is the old salute of that people, which is given to the dead and to the deserving, and in all the annals of the Aelvar it is given to no mortal man more than twice in a century.

“What does it mean?” asked Claus, who was very tired.

“It means,” said Sylwen, “that you should go home, Lord Claus, before the world remembers that it has other uses for you.”

He did not understand her. He wiped his blade upon the ash, and sheathed it, and whistled once, low, through his teeth; and from the baggage-lines, trailing a broken halter and flinging his head at every man who tried to catch him, there came at a trot a great black gelding with a white star the size of a thumbprint upon his brow, whose name was Buro, and who had carried Claus through eleven campaigns and three rivers in flood, and had never, in the opinion of the Levy, carried anyone else without biting him. Claus laid a hand upon the black neck, and looked once at the west, where beyond a hundred leagues of road and forest there stood a house of grey stone with a lamp in its window; and the wind of the Marches, that had blown against him all that day, turned about, and blew at his back.

### Chapter III: The Laurel Without Warmth

The third day of Emberfall, in the year aforesaid; the seventh hour of the evening; in the Hall of Sunlit Pillars, beneath the dome of the Gilded Meridian, at Solhaven.

*From the Sayings of the Gilded Hall, as they were taught to the pages of the court:*

> *A crown of gold is cold to wear,*  
> *A king’s kind smile is colder than the air;*  
> *Who takes the gift and bows the knee*  
> *Is owned by him who sets him free.*

It is four weeks’ ride from the Ashen Marches to Solhaven, and Claus, riding alone upon the black gelding Buro, accomplished it in four-and-twenty days, for he was not a man who loved to be thanked, and the road, in his experience, was shorter than the feast at its end. But the road, upon that journey, was not what it had been. News outruns a horseman; the tidings of the Titan’s fall had gone before him down every lane and ferry of the east, and in each village through which he passed the people came out of their doors and stood along the way in silence, as people stand when a funeral goes by that is also a festival. Garlands of late asters hung from the eaves. Children had been lifted upon their fathers’ shoulders. At a ford upon the river Whitwater, an old woman with a face like a winter apple pushed through the press, and thrust a warm loaf into his saddle-bag, and would not be refused.

“My grandson was at Harrowford when the ash came down in ’27,” she said, “and he never came out of the smoke. You will forgive an old woman for not kneeling. My knees are no better than the king’s justice.”

It was a thing said without malice, and almost without thought, as such things are said by those who have outlived the fear of saying them. Claus, who had been brought up in a smithy and had never learned to bear praise gracefully, thanked her with a clumsiness that made the onlookers smile, and rode on. But the chroniclers, who pick through the small events of a great year as a jeweller sorts seed-pearls, have set this down as the first sign. The people did not love the king. They loved the man who had come out of the smoke. And a king who is not loved, and sees another loved in his stead, is a dangerous creature.

The capital of Aurendel rises in seven terraces from the banks of the river Auren, each wall of white stone set higher than the last, until at the summit of the hill the Gilded Meridian crowns the whole, its great dome of beaten gold so bright in the morning that travellers upon the western road are said to shade their eyes at a distance of ten miles. By the third day of Emberfall, in the year aforesaid, every terrace was hung with banners of azure and gold; the markets of the lower town had been closed for a week; and when the Lord Claus rode in beneath the Gate of the Rising Sun, the noise that went up from the multitude was such that the pigeons of the Meridian rose in a single wheeling cloud, and did not come down again until nightfall.

Beneath the golden dome lies the Hall of Sunlit Pillars, a chamber of ninety columns of pale marble, in which the Sun-Kings of old gave judgment. Its floor is of black and white stone, and across it, from the great doors to the dais, there runs a line of inlaid brass as straight as a plumb-cord, which is the Meridian itself: for at the hour of noon upon the day of the midsummer, the sun, falling through the oculus of the dome, lays a bar of light along that line, and it was the old belief of the dynasty that a king who sat in that light was the sun’s lawful steward upon earth. It is pretty fancy. In the matter of Maldrec it was somewhat strained, for he held all his audiences in the hour after dusk, by torchlight, and was never known to sit in the sun.

At the seventh hour of the evening, when the great clock of the Meridian had struck and the torches were kindled along the ninety columns, the Lord Chamberlain, a plump and nervous gentleman named Ivar Dessen, struck the floor three times with his rod, and called the name in a voice that cracked upon the second syllable.

“Claus! Slayer of Gorrath! Deliverer of the East!”

He came forward alone, along the line of brass, and the nobles drew back from him on either hand like water from a ship’s prow. He wore the mail he had worn upon the field, unburned, for he owned no silk; he had shaved, but not well; and his sword hung at his hip, because a guardsman at the door, seeing the man’s face, had thought better of asking for it. The great ladies fanned themselves and murmured. Along the galleries, where the common people were admitted by the king’s special grace, there was no murmur at all, but a kind of held breath, and Claus, who felt it upon the back of his neck like a warm hand, found that he could not look up.

The king who sat above him was named Maldrec, in the seventy-first year of his age and the thirty-ninth of his reign. He was a lean man, white of beard and slow of speech, whose voice was smooth as oiled steel; and he had come to the throne, according to the court histories of his own reign, by the means called the Restoration of Order, concerning which this chronicle will say more hereafter. Upon his brow he wore the crown of the Sun-Kings, and upon his breast the golden ring that was their emblem. At his left hand, a pace behind the throne, stood a man in a long cloak the colour of ash, bareheaded, his grey hair cropped like a soldier’s, his face as expressionless as a closed door; this was Hartmund, General of the Grey Host, and it is recorded that the nobles near him found, for no reason they could name, that they had begun to speak more quietly. At the king’s right hand, with a flagon of dark Emberfall wine, stood a cupbearer of some fifty years, grey and unremarkable, whose name was Osric.

Maldrec rose, which he was not obliged to do. He descended the three steps of the dais, which no king had done for any subject within the memory of the court; and a sound went through the Hall like the sound of a wind finding a gap in a wall.

“Lord Claus,” said the king. His voice was soft, and carried to the farthest gallery. “You have given this realm what three kings could not: a border without fire. I have known men to be given a province for less. I will not insult you with a province.”

“Sire—”

“I will give you,” said Maldrec, and in his hands there was suddenly a laurel of beaten gold, fine as a woman’s bracelet, that a page had passed to him unseen, “the only thing that is worth giving. I name you Lord Protector of the Realm. You shall have a seat at my council, and command of my armies, and an apartment in this palace, with a window upon the river, where you may sleep as long as you like, and wake to find that nothing is expected of you but that you remain.”

He set the laurel upon Claus’s head. It is noted by one who stood near that the king’s fingers, in that moment, were cold as river-stone, and that Claus, for all the roaring of the hall, felt the cold of them through the gold. He looked up into the king’s face, and saw there was a thing that was not gratitude. It was the look a man gives to a horse he has bought and not yet ridden: patient, appraising, and quite without love. It was gone in a breath. The scribes, who have a gift for such matters, have since called the crown he received that day the Laurel Without Warmth.

The hall waited. A man so honoured is expected to kneel, and swear, and be glad.

“Sire,” said Claus, and the hall grew still, “I thank you. I would be ungrateful to refuse so great a thing without cause, and I have a cause. I have been a sword for twenty years, and it has been a good trade, but a sword is a thing that is always in another man’s hand. I have a wife, whom I have not seen since the spring. I have a daughter who was seven when I left her, and is nine now, and has learned to read, I am told, without my help. I would be a husband and a father, if the realm can spare me.” He drew a breath. “I ask only to leave to go home.”

There was a silence in the Hall of Sunlit Pillars such as the pillars themselves had seldom heard. Somewhere in the gallery a child coughed, and was hushed. The golden laurel, which had seemed so light, grew heavy upon his brow.

“Home,” said Maldrec, softly, as a man tastes an unfamiliar fruit. “And where, Lord Claus, is home?”

“West, Sire. At the edge of the Veyrwood.”

“The Aelvar’s wood.” The king smiled. “I trust you keep good company there.”

It was a small thing, lightly said. But Claus, who had spent twenty years reading the faces of men who wished him dead, felt a coldness go through him that had nothing to do with the autumn air, and he answered, with great care, that his company was a wife and a child and an orchard of pear-trees, and that he had never found them dangerous.

“I do not doubt it.” The king turned, and spread his hands to the court. “The realm,” he said, “is not so poor in swords that it cannot spare one for the hearth!”

And the court, taking its cue as courts will, laughed and applauded, and the feast began.

But there was one in that hall who did not laugh. Osric the cupbearer, who stood at the king’s right hand with the wine, saw, as no other saw, that the royal hand upon the arm of the throne had closed until the knuckles stood white as bone. He saw the king’s head incline a hair’s breadth toward the grey-cloaked general, and he heard, for he was a man whose profession it was to hear and to say nothing, three words murmured so low that they scarcely stirred the air.

“Let him go.”

Hartmund bowed, very low, and said nothing at all; and when he straightened, his eyes, which were the colour of wet slate, rested for a moment upon the departing back of the Hero of the Marches, as a butcher’s eyes rest upon a beast that is not yet ready for the knife.

Osric’s account, which was set down in secret some months afterward and found, long after, among the papers of the Third Cloister, is the sole witness to this exchange, and the chronicler offers it with caution due to all things heard in the shadow of thrones. But it is noted, in the margin of the manuscript, in a different and a trembling hand, that the writer did not sleep that night, and did not pour the king’s wine for a week without his fingers shaking; and the margin adds only this: *I have served a king for twenty years, and had not until that evening understood what he was.*

Claus rode out upon the fourth day of Emberfall, at the first bell of dawn, before the city was awake, mounted upon Buro, who had been stabled among the royal destriers and had bitten two grooms and a chaplain, without farewell and without escort, with the golden laurel wrapped in a rag at the bottom of his saddle-bag, and the old woman’s loaf, grown stale, upon the top of it. The pigeons of the Meridian watched him go. He did not look back, and so did not see the single window, high in the east wing of the palace, where a lamp burned late and a lean white-bearded man stood watching the western road until it was lost in the mist.
